Most of what you’re calling an AI project is a permissions decision

The hard part of an AI project is deciding what it gets to read. Building software doesn’t answer that question, it postpones it.

The meeting was booked for an hour and the project already had a name. An accounting firm wanted an assistant that could answer questions about their document management system — what did we agree to on that engagement, when does the letter renew. They had a vendor, a number, and a slide with a diagram on it.

Twenty minutes in I asked where the working papers actually live. Not where they’re supposed to live. Where they are. There was a pause, and then the answer I’ve now heard at nearly every engagement: the document system has some, a shared drive folder called 2024 Working has most, because checking a file in takes four clicks and nobody does four clicks at eleven at night in March, and a senior manager’s personal drive has the rest.

The tool they were about to buy could have read maybe half of what they wanted to ask it. It would have answered anyway, confidently, and they’d have concluded the AI wasn’t good enough. I told them in that first conversation, before there was a contract, that the project was smaller than the quote and mostly wasn’t software.

The decision under the project

In a professional services firm or a family office, the hard part of almost any proposed AI project is not the model. It’s deciding which accounts get connected to which material, who is allowed to see what comes out, and what happens when the thing is wrong. Those are decisions a firm can only make about itself. They can’t be bought, and they can’t be skipped by building — a build doesn’t answer the access question, it moves it behind a schedule and an invoice.

The work isn’t small. It’s been misdiagnosed. Summarizing a lease, sorting a mailbox, pulling dates out of an engagement letter — nobody is confused about whether those are possible. The hard part is deciding that this assistant reads this folder and not that one, drafts but doesn’t send, and gets switched on for these six people. Widen access and something leaks, and it’s your name. Narrow it and someone can’t do their job, still your name.

So it becomes a build instead. An AI initiative is a fundable line item; a shared drive cleanup is not. And the vendor who says what you need is three seats and two hours in an admin console earns two percent of what the vendor who agrees with you does.

The best evidence for this was written by a company with every reason not to write it. Microsoft ships a feature called Restricted SharePoint Search that limits what an assistant can search to a short list of approved sites. Its own documentation calls it “a short-term solution that gives your organization’s administrators time to review and audit site and file permissions,” and “not intended or scalable for long-term use.” A holding pen, so customers can go fix their permissions first.

Two more that weren’t projects

Composites, both.

A family office wanted AI triage on the general mailbox — the one that gets wire confirmations and an invoice for work on the Nantucket house in the same hour. Today that is an office manager who has read it for six years and holds the routing map in her head. Insurance to the risk person. Anything over some dollar figure to the CFO; she has never written that figure down, and when I asked, she had to think. Anything from counsel gets printed.

What came out of that was a one-page document, written in an hour of asking her questions, that put the routing map on paper. And a decision nobody had noticed was a decision: does the tool get the shared mailbox, a separate object with its own delegated permissions, or her mailbox, which also has her performance review in it. Then whether it can send, or only draft — the most useful single setting in this work, and a checkbox.

Then the one that doesn’t resolve.

A firm wanted AI to turn time entries into billing narratives, because the narratives were bad enough that clients were writing time off. The entries said “review.” They said “call re: matter.” Associates were entering a week of time at seven on Friday from memory. Nothing turns “review” into a defensible narrative, because the information isn’t there. It was gone by Tuesday.

Two ways at that. Change when time is entered, which is a policy, not software. Or let the assistant read what the associate was working from — Thursday’s calendar, Thursday’s sent mail, the document versions from Thursday afternoon. Whether that works has nothing to do with the model. It depends on whether the tool can see Thursday.

Which is an access decision, and not a comfortable one. The firm would be deciding to let a tool read associates’ calendars and sent mail to reconstruct their week. I’ve been in the room where the honest recommendation was: don’t grant that access, and so don’t do the project. Not what anyone had budgeted for.

Four questions, in order

The four questions

Stop at the first yes.

  1. Does someone here already do this by hand? Watch them do it once, then give a general-purpose assistant exactly the material they had open. Most tasks stop here.
  2. If nobody does it, is the material unreachable, or is the work hard? Could a competent new hire, given a login and an afternoon, find it? If yes, it’s a permissions problem in a difficulty costume.
  3. Does the output have to be identical every time, or defensible to an outsider? A form filed with a regulator, a number that lands on a K-1. That justifies real engineering — often deterministic software, which is also a no to AI.
  4. Does the task cross a boundary no vendor spans? Two systems that have to reconcile and nothing connects them. A privilege or residency line that rules out the off-the-shelf option. This is the honest yes.

Almost nothing reaches the fourth question on a first pass. Some do. I’ve built the join across an investment platform, an accounting file, bank portals and a document store for a family office, because nobody sells that join and the reconciliation lived in a spreadsheet with tabs named after cousins. The first month wasn’t code. It was deciding who could see the consolidated view, which is more sensitive than any of its inputs. Building didn’t retire the permissions question. It raised the stakes.

The two objections I get

”Our data is too sensitive to connect anything.” The best objection there is. A firm holding privileged communications or a family’s tax positions has duties a SOC 2 report doesn’t discharge. But the material is almost certainly connected already, to accounts the firm doesn’t control. MIT’s NANDA report last year found roughly 40% of companies had bought an official subscription, while workers at more than 90% of companies surveyed reported regular use of personal AI accounts for work. Small survey, self-reported, argued over. That gap matches what I see.

Connect nothing and connect everything aren’t the two options, either. One folder rather than the drive. A shared mailbox rather than a personal one. Read rather than send. A vendor that says it needs your entire Drive is usually describing its own convenience. Sometimes the answer really is no — as a decision with a boundary, though, not an anxiety that blocks the sanctioned tools while permitting whatever is on people’s phones.

In Google’s admin console, third-party app access ships set to, verbatim, “Allow users to access any third-party apps (default) — Users can sign in with Google to any third-party app. Accessed apps can request unrestricted Google data for that user.” Any employee, any app, no administrator in the loop. There’s a checkbox that fixes it.

”You’re a consultant telling us not to hire consultants. Isn’t that the oldest move there is?” Yes, and it has sold a great deal of work. I’m not claiming to be the exception. Judge it by what it costs the person saying it — whether he scopes down in the first conversation, before a contract exists, when it costs him money and gets him nothing.

There’s a business here, and it isn’t charity. Access decisions, configuration, training, reading someone else’s vendor quote: real work, billed by the hour. The claim was never that you need no help. It’s that the help you need is smaller than what you’re being quoted, and I’d rather have the small engagement and the referral than the big one and the resentment. The four questions are meant to be run without me, by whoever knows where things actually live.

The firms that did the access work first found their AI projects got smaller. Sometimes down to a subscription and an afternoon. The firms that built first found the same decision waiting at the end of the build, with more at stake and less patience left in the room.
