How to tell if the AI actually saved anyone time

Eleven people each saving ninety minutes a week adds up to a full-time person, and nobody at the firm can find her.

Eleven people say the new assistant saves them about ninety minutes a week. Nobody is inflating anything; ask any of them to name the task and they can. Add the eleven up and you have a full-time person. Now go find her. The firm hasn’t taken on more matters, nobody’s recorded hours went down, and the invoices are the same size as last year.

That arithmetic is free, and it is the cheapest test of whether two numbers you already believe can both be true. Then I ask what those numbers looked like before anyone logged in. Usually nobody pulled them, and that one can’t be fixed later.

Nobody has to be lying

Wins are events: the memo that appeared in four minutes has a clean before and after, and it stays with you. Losses are weather — twenty minutes last Tuesday re-prompting a summary that kept dropping the counterparty’s name, in three-minute pieces, none of which felt like using the AI so much as like working. And the abandoned attempts never reach the answer: it works on three tasks in ten, you finish the other seven the old way, and your month-end answer is about the three.

None of this requires anyone to be dishonest. Google’s DORA researchers modeled AI adoption across software organizations in 2024 and found individual and system outcomes moving opposite ways: for every 25% increase in adoption, individual flow rose an estimated 2.6% and job satisfaction 2.2%, while delivery throughput fell an estimated 1.5% and delivery stability 7.2%.1 Everyone reporting that the work feels better is telling the truth. So is the system. No survey of individuals could have found the second half.

The best reason to distrust your own read is a study of sixteen experienced developers on 246 real tasks in repositories they’d worked in for years. Beforehand they expected AI to make them 24% faster. Afterward, having done the work, they estimated 20% faster. Measured, they took 19% longer.2 The slowdown isn’t the interesting part. Finishing the work didn’t correct the belief.

Two things about it, or the rest of this post is hypocrisy. It’s developers on open-source code, not lawyers on matters, and what transfers is the mechanism — nobody can observe their own counterfactual speed. And the same group published a follow-up in February: a second experiment came out the other way, they called their own newer evidence “very weak,” and they changed the design.3 The argument was never that AI makes people slower. It’s that people can’t tell.

Which is why I won’t repeat the figure you’ve heard most, about 95% of enterprise AI pilots returning nothing: a few dozen executive interviews, about 150 survey responses, data never released.4 It gets quoted to me by people who would never accept a vendor’s number on those terms.

Self-report is a compass, not a ruler: it tells you where people believe the value is, and it’s the only honest instrument for whether the work feels less grinding. Ask what the last task was and what they did with the output, not how much time it saved.

The measurement you can only take once

Almost everything you need is already in a system you pay for. Time entries with task codes. Matter open and close dates. Preparer hours against reviewer hours. Write-downs. The client request in, and the answer that actually answered it out.

Before the first login is issued

Export twelve to twenty-four months of it into a folder that won’t change, and write down what each metric means the same day — turnaround on a lease abstract measured from the client’s email, or from when it hit the assignee’s queue; pick one. An afternoon. It’s the only item in this post with no downside and the only one that becomes impossible later.

Twelve months rather than four weeks: you need tax season and the summer lull in there. A “before” of March against an “after” of July compares AI to the calendar. And measure work, not people: how long a lease abstract takes us and how fast Sarah is are different sentences, and staff hear the difference from down the hall.

Comparisons a twenty-person firm can make

You aren’t running an experiment. You’re running a comparison, and it reduces to two questions: compared to what, and did you decide before you saw the answer.

So don’t switch it on for twenty people on one Monday. Group A in month one, B in month two, C in month three, each group a control for the one ahead of it. The largest credible field study of a real deployment, 5,179 customer support agents, used a staggered rollout because that’s how software gets turned on anyway.5 Set the order for a reason unrelated to who will benefit: alphabetical, by office, coin flip. The instinct is to start with your most enthusiastic person, and that destroys the comparison — the enthusiast was going to look better regardless. Start there if adoption pressure demands it, but then that person’s numbers are not evidence.

Then measure twice. In the first weeks people are excited, they know they’re being watched, and they’re working on the tasks the tool is best at. All three change, while skill pushes the other way. A single measurement of a changing thing is not a measurement. Look at ninety days, then again at a hundred and eighty.

Time saved is not time captured

This is the part most people selling you something won’t say out loud.

An associate gets meaningfully faster on the third of her work that’s drafting. Those hours don’t get billed. That comes off the top line, not out of costs — you paid for the seats, then again in revenue you no longer invoice. A real efficiency gain is a revenue event before it’s a cost event.

The deck assumes it away. Thomson Reuters’ 2024 survey — serious, honestly labeled a prediction — has professionals expecting AI to save four hours a week within a year, and converts that, for a U.S. lawyer, into “an estimated $100,000 in additional billable hours.”6 Which holds only if every recovered hour refills with billable work.

Sometimes it does. More often it goes elsewhere. More matters, if there’s demand at the margin, which most twenty-person firms can’t switch on inside a quarter — if the work were there they’d have taken it. The work quietly expanding to fill the space, so the memo gets longer and the review gets one more pass and no operational number moves. Fewer nights, real and invisible in revenue and visible in retention. Or a seat you don’t backfill.

Those live in different places, so pick one before you start. More matters means watching matters opened; fewer nights means watching hours worked and not being disappointed that revenue is flat. Otherwise you’ll measure whichever number moved and call that the plan.

The best evidence I can hand a managing partner is a randomized study rather than a vendor. Choi, Monahan and Schwarcz ran law students through four legal tasks with and without an AI assistant, blind-graded. Time fell on all four, between 11.8% and 32.1%. Quality barely moved: +0.17, +0.24, +0.07 and −0.07 on a four-point scale, only one holding up.7 Speed and quality don’t travel together. Then, in their limitations section — the students were paid a flat rate, so they wanted to finish:

“In the real world, under time-based client billing, lawyers have an economic incentive to spend as much time as possible on a task in order to maximize revenue. It may be, therefore, that our findings regarding efficiency will not translate to real world settings.”

A randomized study conceding that the billable hour may erase its own finding. Which suggests why so few firms measure this at all: nobody wants to prove the purchase cost them money. The absence of measurement is sometimes a decision.

What a control group is actually for

I spent years building the machinery that tells a company whether a change did anything — experiment infrastructure, KPI definitions, the modeling layer everybody’s numbers came out of. The hardest conversations were never about statistics. They were with people who had watched a number move and wanted to know why a holdout was still necessary. The answer never changes. Numbers move.

So fix the metric before you look at the data, because if you choose afterward you’ll choose the one that moved — not out of dishonesty, out of narrative. Watch twelve numbers for a quarter and one will have improved. Then decide in advance what result would make you stop: if total hours per lease abstract haven’t fallen by day 90, we drop it. That removes the negotiation that happens later, when the number disappoints and somebody says it’s still early and the benefits are qualitative and the team likes it. All of that may be true. If it would have been said regardless of the result, it isn’t evidence. And you have to be willing to stop; a rule you override teaches everyone the exercise was theater.

The reason to do any of this isn’t to prove the tool works. A firm that knows how long its own work takes is better run whatever it decides about AI, and the baseline outlives the tool, the vendor, and the enthusiasm.

A fair measurement will sometimes come back saying it helped a little, and less than you paid for it, and that the people using it are noticeably happier anyway. There’s no metric that turns that into a win unless you decided in advance that it was one. I’ve had that conversation and I expect to have it again. It goes better when the sentence was written down in February.
