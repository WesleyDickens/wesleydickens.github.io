What a family office should actually do about AI

Most of the AI question in a family office is a question about who can read what, and nobody has looked in years.

I asked a six-person family office who could read the principal’s email.

The answer took a week. It arrived assembled out of three people’s memories and one screenshot of an admin console nobody had opened since the tenant was set up. Five names. One of them had left the office two years earlier.

Nothing had gone wrong. No breach, no incident, no reason for anyone to have looked. The office was running fine, which is the trouble with access questions — they cost nothing until they cost everything, and they never surface on their own. Usually what forces them is a tool somebody wants to connect.

I’ve written before that most of what gets called an AI project is really a permissions decision. In a family office that isn’t a framing device, it’s the job, because the office was never built with a seam between the business and the household to begin with.

Not a small company

Six people is a real office: a COO, a controller, an investment-operations person, an executive assistant, a property manager, someone part-time on bill pay. Tax, legal, custody and IT are outsourced. Capital calls, the CPA’s forty questions, tuition and club dues and the wine account, four properties with contractors and staff, the foundation, the children — all of it running at once and interrupting itself.

From the outside this looks like a twenty-person software company. It isn’t.

Confidentiality is the product. A company that leaks its roadmap has a bad quarter. An office that leaks a trust structure, a property address, or a child’s school has created a permanent, searchable, personal fact, and in some families a physical-security problem.

There’s no IT department. There’s a guy. He set up the Wi-Fi at the house and he also administers the tenant, and nobody has looked at permissions since it was created. No procurement function, no security team, no one whose job is to say no.

Personal and business data are intermingled by design. The same drive holds the ground lease, the pool contractor’s invoice, the estate plan, and a scan of a passport. The same mailbox handles a capital call and a dinner reservation. There is no clean line to cut along, which is why just point us at your drive is a dangerous sentence.

And the family itself is an operating constraint. A tool the principal likes and the children distrust won’t survive. A project pitched as efficiency gets read by staff as a headcount conversation. Offices decline good, cheap tools over one-in-a-thousand scenarios, and they aren’t being irrational — the downside is unbounded and personal. Anyone who treats that as ignorance deserves to be shown out.

The assistant’s mailbox

The executive assistant’s mailbox is usually the most sensitive surface in the office. It’s also the first thing anyone wants to connect a tool to, because it’s where the drudgery is.

What’s in it: delegated full access to the principal’s mailbox, granted years ago and never reviewed, so that anything connected as the assistant reads the principal’s mail. Send-as rights, so that anything able to send can send as the principal. Calendar entries naming doctors, lawyers and therapists — the calendar leaks the relationships even when the content sits elsewhere. School correspondence about minors. Travel itineraries, which are the family’s physical location on specific dates. Wire instructions pasted into threads. Credentials pasted into threads in 2017 and never rotated. And a great deal of ordinary family correspondence with no business character at all.

So audit the delegation before you connect anything.

Before anything connects
  • Full access grants on every mailbox, including the ones nobody uses
  • Send-as and send-on-behalf rights
  • Calendar delegates and auto-forwarding rules
  • Legacy shared mailboxes
  • Files set to “anyone with the link”

Nearly every office I’ve done this with turns up at least one delegation nobody can explain.

Then don’t connect that mailbox at all. Stand up a dedicated address, route to it the mail you actually want help with: custodian statements, invoices, capital call notices. Connect the tool to that. Nearly the same value, and the exposure is a rounding error next to the alternative.

An AI assistant doesn’t have access. The account it uses has access. At the consent screen, have someone read the permissions out loud. “Read mail in all mailboxes” and “read files in this one site” are different sentences, and only one of them is a decision anybody meant to make.

Documents work the same way. Don’t point a tool at the existing drive and exclude the folders you’d rather it not see. Build a new location and move the allowed material in. Allowlist, not blocklist — exclusion lists rot, because every folder created next quarter is included by default and nobody who creates one reads the policy.

Where it doesn’t go

Approvals stay on for anything that sends, shares, or deletes. That’s the principle. The rest is a list.

  • Payment initiation. Nothing near the wire path. Not initiating, not approving, not pre-filling for review. Callback verification stays human and out of band, on a number from your own records. A face and a voice on a video call stopped being a control a couple of years ago, when a finance employee joined one with a synthetic CFO and moved eight figures.
  • Anything that sends externally without a human. Email, portal messages, calendar invites, e-signature requests. The drafts folder is the boundary.
  • Minors. School records, medical, custody arrangements. Not carefully. Not at all.
  • Family medical matters — the principal’s, the spouse’s, the children’s, the household staff’s.
  • Tax positions. Not deductibility, not basis, not entity classification, not whether it’s a wash sale. A K-1 gets read. It does not get interpreted.
  • Operative documents. Trust amendments, side letters, employment agreements, NDAs. Reviewing someone else’s draft is fine. Producing text that gets signed is not.
  • Anything a court, counterparty, regulator, or carrier reads. The legal profession has run that experiment in public for three years, and courts have addressed invented citations in well over a thousand cases.

What actually binds you

Most single family offices sit outside investment-adviser registration through the family office exclusion. So the constraints here are contractual and reputational rather than regulatory — a different set, not a lighter one, and much harder to look up.

Read the confidentiality clause in your fund documents before an LPA goes into anything. Ask counsel which files counsel considers privileged and keep those out of scope; that’s one email and an hour. Whether handing a privileged document to a vendor under confidentiality terms waives privilege is genuinely unsettled, and I’m not going to tell you it’s fine. Whether the federal safeguards rules reach an unregistered office is less obvious than either side of that argument sounds. Ask your lawyer, not a consultant and not a vendor.

Then the test that actually governs: would the family be comfortable reading a plain description of the arrangement? Stricter than any rule above, and the right one.

The cloud objection usually arrives around here, and it deserves a real answer. Being more conservative than a corporate IT department is rational here, not ignorant. But Deloitte’s 2024 survey of 354 single family offices found 87 percent already running cloud applications. The custodian portal is cloud. The accounting system is cloud. The mail is cloud. That was settled years ago by whoever configured the tenant, and nobody wrote it down as a decision. The live question is which vendors, on what terms, reaching what.

Some material should go to no vendor at all. The estate plan. The medical file. Anything about the children. A local option for that narrow set exists, it’s slower, and the output is worse. For a dozen documents a year, worse is fine.

Start embarrassingly small

The right first project for a six-person office is small enough to be faintly embarrassing: reading documents the office already has and already trusts. The eighty-page LPA nobody read. The umbrella policy with three riders. The ground lease. No connections at all — people upload the document they’re working on. That is the whole configuration.

It answers the only question that matters this early: whether these five people will use it. Citi’s May 2026 work on family offices found the barrier offices cite most often is a lack of internal expertise. A six-person office has no slack. If the first project requires anyone to learn a platform, it’s dead by week three.

After that, one connection, narrowly scoped, to a location built for the purpose. Then one workflow with a person in the loop — renewal dates proposed into a tracker the COO already reviews. At ninety days, look at who actually used it.

I build this kind of thing for a living, so weigh what follows accordingly. What changes the office isn’t the tool. It’s the two weeks spent finding out who can read what — work the office should have done anyway, and mostly only does because a tool forced the question.

The AI is the excuse. The audit is the deliverable.
