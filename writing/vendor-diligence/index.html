A diligence checklist for AI vendors

Ask for evidence of how the product handles your documents, then decide whether the terms fit the work.

A partner forwarded me a vendor’s security packet and asked whether it was any good. It had forty pages on encryption, physical security, and the data center. It did not explain where the firm’s client documents would sit inside the product, who could read them, or what happened when someone hit delete.

Those were the questions we still needed answered. A security packet can be useful without covering the particular use you have in mind.

A clear answer makes a vendor’s terms easier to evaluate. It can also establish that the product is unsuitable. “Thirty days in backups” is specific, but the firm still has to decide whether it can accept thirty days. Give the vendor time to consult the people responsible and ask for documents you can keep with the decision.

Four requests to start with
  1. Is our content used to train any model on the plan we would buy? Ask for the default, any relevant setting, and the contractual commitment. If training on this material is unacceptable, confirm that the purchased plan meets that requirement.
  2. Which other providers receive our content? Request the model provider, subprocessor list, processing locations, and applicable terms. Check them against the restrictions on the material you intend to submit.
  3. Who at your company can read our content? Ask how access is approved and logged, including support and incident handling. Decide whether those arrangements are acceptable for the files in scope.
  4. Send the SOC 2 Type II report and the clause covering model training. Check that they apply to the product and plan under consideration. If the vendor cannot provide a report, decide what additional evidence you would need before proceeding.

Keep the written answers with the agreement and the version of the terms you reviewed. Record any gaps and who accepted them. A quick reply is convenient; the documents and their applicability are what support the decision.

What to do with an answer

Read the documents against the proposed use

Product tiers and settings. Consumer and business plans can differ on training and retention. Identify the plan the staff doing the work will actually use, including personal accounts. Ask whether the promise depends on a setting and what its default is. A firm’s agreement does not establish the terms of an employee’s separate account.

SOC 2. Request the report rather than relying on “SOC 2 compliant” in a sales presentation. A Type I report addresses controls at a point in time; a Type II also covers their operation over a period. Review the product scope, report period, criteria covered, and any exceptions. Check whether Confidentiality is included and whether the AI features fall inside the examined system.2

Changes to terms. Read how the vendor may change the agreement and what notice you receive. Ask counsel to review how those provisions interact with negotiated commitments, termination rights, and your obligations to clients. A confidentiality promise is less useful if the available remedy does not cover the risk you are accepting; review the liability cap and any relevant exclusions too.

Discontinuation. Look for the notice and transition terms if the product or a feature goes away. Check whether preview features are covered. An indemnity covering certain claims about output answers a different question from how long a service will remain available. Ask what is contractually promised and what remains a statement of current plans. Use the answer to decide how much of the workflow you are willing to make dependent on the product.

Common terms that still need a decision

A provision can be common and still conflict with the proposed use. Ask what it permits before deciding whether you can accept it.

  • Subprocessor changes. If the vendor can add or replace providers, check how it gives notice and what options you have when a new provider is unacceptable.
  • Human review and feedback. Ask whether flagged content or a submitted thumbs-down can send a transcript to a reviewer. Check whether the no-training commitment also covers those paths. Staff need to understand what the feedback button shares.
  • Regional routing. Find out which processing locations the contract permits. Compare that boundary with any restrictions on the documents.
  • Single sign-on. If it requires a higher tier, include that tier in the comparison. Otherwise, decide who will manage separate accounts and revoke access when someone leaves.

Encryption at rest does not answer who can read content through an authorized application or account. Keep the encryption question, and ask about staff access and account controls separately.

Follow-up questions for the people running the service

  • “Which model, which provider, hosted where?” Ask for names and locations, or a list if routing varies. If a component is proprietary, ask what information the vendor can provide about processing and data use. Record what you still cannot assess.
  • “Are your model-provider terms standard or negotiated?” Ask which terms support the promises made to you. Check for differences between what the vendor offers and what its provider commits to. Using standard terms alone does not establish that the product is unsuitable.
  • “What happens between our browser and the model?” Request an explanation covering where documents are stored, retrieved, logged, and deleted. Have the vendor involve its technical team where necessary. Use the description to check that the written answers cover every place the content goes.
  • “What uses do you advise customers against?” Ask for documented limitations and how they affect your intended workflow. If the answer leaves your main use unresolved, arrange a test or narrow the scope before purchase.

Check what your own deployment changes

The vendor’s answers apply to a particular product and arrangement. They do not decide which files you should connect or how staff should handle the output.

  • Define the connection scope. Use the document and mailbox scoping questions to decide what the tool needs.
  • Review existing permissions. A tool that follows them can still expose material to someone whose access should have been removed.
  • Check the accounts used by the people handling documents. Buying an enterprise plan for partners does not cover associates using personal accounts.
  • Decide where work product belongs. A chat history may not be covered by the matter file’s retention and review procedures.
  • Assign someone to review changes and renewals. Keep the approved scope and outstanding questions with that record.

Keep the review proportionate to the material

A small firm may not have a procurement team. It can still send the four requests, retain the answers, and decide which material the arrangement permits. If a question remains unresolved, keep the affected files out of the deployment while it is resolved.

I’m not a lawyer. For legal practices, the ABA’s Formal Opinion 512 is a useful starting point for discussing the review with counsel: it calls on lawyers to understand the relevant terms and who can access information submitted to a tool, or consult someone who has analyzed them.1

Write the decision in terms someone can use later: this plan is approved for these documents and these tasks, subject to the listed conditions. That gives the person handling the next client file something more useful than a folder marked “vendor approved.”
