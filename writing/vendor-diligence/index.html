A diligence checklist for AI vendors

Most of these questions have no wrong answer, only a vague one. The useful part is knowing how to hear the reply.

A partner forwarded me a vendor’s security packet and asked whether it was any good. Forty pages on encryption at rest, encryption in transit, and physical security at a data center the vendor doesn’t own. It answered every question you’d put to a payroll provider. It never said where the firm’s client documents would sit once they went into the product, who at the vendor could read them, or what happened when somebody hit delete.

The packet wasn’t dishonest. It was answering a question from 2016.

Here’s the list I use instead. Take it, put your own name on it, hand it to whoever runs your IT.

One thing before you start, because it changes how you hear the answers. On most of these there is no wrong answer, only a vague one. You’re measuring how much of their own system a vendor can describe without looking it up. A crisp, inconvenient answer — “thirty days in the backups, and no, we can’t get it out faster” — means somebody has thought about it. A vendor who answers a specific question with a values statement hasn’t, and will do it again on the next one.

The short version

If you ask four questions and nothing else, ask these. Send them in one email and keep the reply.

  1. Is my content used to train any model — on the plan I’m actually buying — and is that commitment in the contract or on a webpage? Three answers in one: the default, the tier, and whether the promise is enforceable.
  2. Whose computers does my content touch besides yours, and what are your contracts with them? Produces the model provider, the subprocessor list, and where inference happens.
  3. Who at your company can read my content, and is that access logged? Almost nobody asks. The answer is diagnostic even when it’s good.
  4. Send me your SOC 2 Type II report and the clause where you commit not to train on my data. How fast these come back tells you as much as what’s in them.

Ask in writing. In a year, when the terms have quietly changed, that email is the only record of what you were promised. Stop reading here and you’ve got most of the value.

What a good answer sounds like

The questionA real answerThe non-answer that gets written down
Is my content used to train any model?“No. Section 4.2 of the MSA, and it covers the plan you’re buying.”“We take privacy extremely seriously.” Or “we never sell your data” — nobody asked about selling.
How long do you keep prompts and files, and what happens when I delete?Separate numbers for their database, their model provider, and their logs, plus an outer bound for backups.“As long as necessary to provide the service.” Or “deleted instantly” — nobody’s backups work that way.
Who at your company can read my content?“No standing access. A ticket, time-boxed, logged, and we’ll show you the log.”“Only authorized personnel.” Everyone is authorized personnel.

The tier gap. Consumer and business tiers of the same product routinely differ on whether content is used for training. That’s normal, disclosed, and not a scandal. But it means your real exposure isn’t the contract you signed. It’s the personal account somebody set up because the firm tool is slower. Ask which tiers differ, and whether accounts on your email domain get pulled into your tenant automatically. Ask whether the promise is a default or a setting — “a switch in admin settings” means default on.

SOC 2. There is no SOC 2 certification, and “SOC 2 compliant” isn’t a status. A Type I says the controls were designed properly on one day. A Type II says they operated over a period. Ask for the Type II under NDA, then open it, which almost nobody does. Check that the scope covers the product you’re buying, because AI features are often outside it. Check that Confidentiality is one of the criteria, since it’s optional and often missing. Read the exceptions: every real report has some, and zero is worth a question.

The clause that undoes the others. Somewhere in a self-serve agreement is a line saying the vendor may modify the terms by posting revised terms. It quietly unwinds everything above it, because every promise you negotiated is held in place by a document the other side can rewrite while you sleep. Ask that material changes require notice and a right to terminate. Absent from most self-serve contracts, standard in real enterprise ones, and the redline most worth spending your goodwill on. While you’re in there, carve confidentiality breach out of the liability cap. Twelve months of fees is market standard and is no remedy for a leaked client file.

What they won’t promise. Read the indemnity clause, then go looking for the discontinuation clause. The pattern across the major vendors’ published terms is that they’ll defend you against a copyright claim over the output and commit to nothing at all about keeping the product running — the strongest notice I’ve found in standard terms is twelve months, several have no such provision, and preview features are carved out of it everywhere. So ask what notice you get before a product or a feature you depend on goes away, and whether it’s in the contract or in a blog post. “We have no plans to discontinue it” is a statement about this week. You can’t diligence your way to knowing whether a vendor survives; you can find out cheaply what happens if it doesn’t, and unlike their runway, that part is negotiable.

This sounds alarming and is completely normal

Telling the difference is most of the work. None of the following is a finding.

  • “We may update our subprocessors from time to time.” Standard language in most data processing agreements. Ask about the notice mechanism, not the clause.
  • Human review of a sample of flagged content, and a thumbs-down button that ships the transcript to a reviewer. Both are common, disclosed, and usually outside the no-training promise, which makes that button the likeliest accidental disclosure route in an otherwise correct deployment. Tell your people what it does.
  • Cross-region routing for capacity within a geography. What matters is whether the boundary is contractual, not whether every request lands in one building.
  • Single sign-on priced on a higher tier. Irritating, widespread, and a pricing decision rather than a security signal. Price it in: without SSO, offboarding depends on somebody remembering to open an admin panel, and the person who left still knows the password.

One correction, since it comes up constantly: encryption at rest protects you from someone stealing a disk, not from the vendor’s own staff or a compromised account inside your firm. It leads every security questionnaire and tells you almost nothing.

Questions where vagueness is the answer

These cost ninety seconds each and need no preparation.

  • ”Which model, which provider, hosted where?” One sentence with proper nouns in it. Or “a proprietary blend of leading models,” which is a wrapper that would rather not say.
  • ”Is your agreement with your model provider negotiated, or the standard developer terms?” Both are legitimate. But if it’s the standard terms, every promise they’ve made you rests on a click-through they had no hand in writing, and they should say so without flinching. Then ask for the clause itself. A serious vendor sends it that afternoon; a wrapper sends a marketing page three days later.
  • ”Walk me through what happens between my browser and the model.” A serious vendor describes a queue, a database, a retrieval step, a logging boundary. A wrapper describes the user interface.
  • ”What’s the last thing you told a customer not to use this for?” A vendor with real deployments answers immediately. A vendor selling a demo doesn’t.

When every answer is fine and the deployment is still wrong

Diligence answers one question: is this vendor safe to hand material to. It doesn’t answer which material, whose, or who’s accountable. Nearly everything I get called about after a bad rollout is on this list.

  • You pointed it at everything. Nothing in the vendor’s terms caused that; the connection scope did, which is most of what an AI project actually is.
  • The tool faithfully respects your document permissions, and your permissions have been wrong for six years, because nothing could search all of it at once until now. An assistant is an excellent audit tool for access problems you didn’t know you had, and a terrible way to find out about them.
  • You bought the enterprise tier for twelve partners. The forty associates doing the document work are on the free tier on their phones, so your contract covers the people generating the least risk.
  • Work product now lives in a chat history. It isn’t in the matter file, isn’t covered by your retention policy, and won’t surface on a conflicts check or a hold.
  • Nobody owns it. Every answer verified, tool deployed, nobody responsible for looking again in a year, in a category where terms change unilaterally. Somebody’s name goes on the renewal review or the diligence was theater.

The checklist protects you from the vendor. Most of what goes wrong isn’t the vendor.

Doing less of this

If you run a twelve-person firm, putting a procurement process around a thirty-dollar-a-seat tool is a category error, not prudence. I’d rather you asked the four questions properly than the forty badly. What doesn’t scale down with headcount is your duty to the client. You can also narrow the deployment until the diligence you can afford is enough for the material in it. You don’t need a complete answer to start. You need one before the client files go in.

I’m not a lawyer and this isn’t legal advice. But the American Bar Association wrote something in 2024 that reads like an instruction to run a list of this kind:

As a baseline, all lawyers should read and understand the Terms of Use, privacy policy, and related contractual terms and policies of any GAI tool they use to learn who has access to the information that the lawyer inputs into the tool or consult with a colleague or external expert who has read and analyzed those terms and policies.

The vendor’s promise runs to you. Your duty runs to your client. Two different chains, and nothing the vendor signs moves your obligation onto theirs.
