Your AI pilot didn’t fail. Your data did.

A partner told me the tool had hallucinated. It hadn’t. It had quoted a retired policy correctly, from the folder where the current one lives.

A partner told me her firm’s assistant had hallucinated. She had asked about one of the firm’s internal policies, the answer came back wrong, and she knew it was wrong because she had written the current policy herself the previous spring.

We opened the file it cited. It had quoted the document word for word — right sentence, right paragraph, right file. The file was the 2019 version, sitting in the same folder as the current one, because nobody deletes a policy. They write a new one and save it next to the old one.

That is a filing failure, and it had been a filing failure since 2019. The only thing that changed is that something finally read the folder.

Some version of that afternoon happens at nearly every engagement I take. The pilot didn’t fail. It worked. It went and got the answer the firm’s data actually contains, and nobody liked the answer.

Two departments, one word

Finance’s “client” is a billing entity. The partners’ “client” is a relationship — a family, a founder, a group of related LLCs. Marketing’s “client” is anyone in the CRM with a closed-won deal. Three counts, three reconciliation meetings a year, and by now everyone has privately settled the disagreement in their own head and stopped bringing it up.

In the reporting era that argument got settled once, out loud. Building a report forced somebody to write down which definition it used and defend the choice in a room. The answer was arbitrary, it stuck, and every number after it inherited it. An assistant with access to all three systems settles it at run time instead. Silently. Differently depending on who asks and how they phrase it. Two partners can ask how many clients the firm has an hour apart and get two different numbers, and nothing in either answer shows where the choice was made.

A dashboard fails toward a blank. A model fails toward a paragraph. When a pipeline broke, a chart went to zero and somebody said that’s wrong and went looking. Hand a model the same broken input and it produces fluent, well-organized, correctly-cited prose, and nobody says that’s wrong because nothing looks wrong.

The readership changed too. A report had a few consumers who knew the number’s caveats. A chat box has everyone in the firm who can type, none of whom know what happened underneath, all of whom will forward the answer to a client. These tools didn’t create the definition problem. They removed the last person who was translating.

It can’t tell you what it didn’t find

The mechanism, once, plainly: documents get cut into pieces, each piece is turned into a numerical fingerprint, your question gets the same treatment, and the system hands the model whichever pieces sit nearest yours. Everything that follows is a way in which the nearest pieces are not the pieces that answer the question.

Nothing in a file store marks a document as dead. A superseded policy has the same file type and similar text, and it often scores a better match than the live one, which may be shorter or phrased differently or trapped in a PDF that parsed badly. Recency isn’t part of similarity, and modified dates don’t rescue you — a file’s date changes when somebody opens it and re-saves it, so the 2018 policy a paralegal touched last March looks newer than the 2024 one. When the model states the retired clause in the present tense, it cites a real document that really does say that. The citation makes it more believable, not less.

A PDF can be a document or a photograph of a document. To an indexer, a scanned page with no text layer is an empty file: no error, zero characters, gone from every search result. And the scans are systematically the important ones — executed agreements, signature pages, letters from counsel, anything that arrived from outside as a fax or a photo of a signed page. Born-digital documents index perfectly. Legally operative ones often don’t.

Then there’s everything your folder structure knows. /Clients/Former/ versus /Clients/Active/. A blank template and the signed version of the same agreement are nearly identical as text; the only thing separating them is which folder they sit in, and chunking throws the folder away. Your Archive and Do Not Use directories are the firm’s real governance and the index cannot see a character of it.

Permissions have their own post — most of what you’re calling an AI project is a permissions decision. The data-specific part: folders that were safe because nobody knew they existed are not safe against a box that answers questions. Two years ago Microsoft shipped a switch to turn organization-wide search off for its own assistant, describing it as a stopgap while administrators review site permissions.1 Vendors don’t often ship features that make their product see less.

One thing did genuinely get better. The meaning in most firms lives in free text, in the notes field where somebody typed spoke with Jim, board meets in May, revisit after, and these tools can read it. But extraction is a hypothesis, not a fact, and it goes wrong most often on the terse notes written in shorthand by the busiest people. Keep the source text beside the extracted field and check a sample by hand.

Questions to ask before you buy anything

None of these needs a technical person, and how hard they are to answer is itself the answer.

  1. Write down three real sentences. Not use cases — questions somebody would actually type. Which clients haven’t we billed this quarter. If you can’t produce three, you don’t have a project yet, you have an interest.
  2. Ask three people to define your central noun. Client, matter, engagement, household. Separately, in writing, one sentence each, no conferring. If the sentences differ, a tool will pick one and never mention that it chose.
  3. Pick one real client and count how many times they exist. Billing, CRM, document store, email, the spreadsheet. Note the spellings. Then answer the question nobody has ever written down: which system is right when they disagree?
  4. Ask what one important field’s value was a year ago. A status, a fee arrangement, a rate. Actually try to find out. If you can’t, every question starting how often or is this getting better is unanswerable no matter what you buy. Start a nightly snapshot of four or five tables today; it costs almost nothing and it isn’t a project.
  5. Open the folder where the answer should live and look at the last twenty files. Count the near-duplicates and the ones with a version number in the name. Open a scan and try to select the text with your cursor. If you can’t select it, no software can read it.
  6. Ask how you would know if it were wrong. Not “would we notice.” What is the check, who runs it, and what happens the first time it fails. The bad answer is “people would tell us.” They won’t. They’ll quietly stop using it.

What this is not an argument for

It is not an argument for a data warehouse. I’ve built them, which is what lets me say this: a warehouse is a place to put agreed-upon facts, and if the agreement never happened, it’s the same argument in a nicer schema.

Split the work everyone calls cleaning. Deduplicating files, running OCR, keeping an archive folder out of the index — that belongs inside the project. It’s scopeable and it ends. What a client is, which system wins, whether the 2019 fee arrangement still applies to three people: that is governance wearing cleaning’s clothes, and no project team can do it, because no project team has the authority. The tell is whether the blocker is somebody’s afternoon or somebody’s calendar. Get those decisions made in one meeting before anything starts. There will be fewer than ten of them.

Then make the question smaller. Every firm has one corner that’s already tidy — a practice group that files properly, a system somebody actually maintains. Ship something people use in that corner. Don’t clean the drive; index the folder that is already clean. None of this is a character flaw, incidentally. The messiest drives I see belong to the busiest and most profitable firms.

If you want to know whether you’re being sold to, ask what they’d cut. When every answer to “our data is messy” is a bigger engagement, you have your answer.

A failed pilot is diagnostic equipment. What most firms get out of one is a precise, expensive, six-week demonstration of which of their own facts they don’t agree on. That was worth knowing. It would have been cheaper to learn it in a two-hour meeting with the four people who have been there longest, arguing about what “active” means — which is what you were always going to be arguing about.
