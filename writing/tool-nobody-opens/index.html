The tool nobody opens

A rollout can go perfectly and still end with nobody opening the thing. Usually the people who stopped were right to stop.

Six weeks after a rollout I sat down next to a senior associate and asked her to show me the last thing she had used the tool for. She opened it, scrolled the history, and the most recent item was the two of us, in the training session, working through a document she had brought. Nothing after that. She wasn’t embarrassed and had no reason to be. Her reason was a good one and it took about four minutes to find.

The software worked. The logins worked, the license was paid, and for a month afterward the vendor’s dashboard counted her as an active user. Nobody used it. That is the ordinary outcome of a competent rollout. The useful question isn’t why people resist; it’s what calculation they ran that came out negative.

Why people stop

Mostly the tool is a detour. The work lives in an inbox, a document system, a spreadsheet, and opening the tool means leaving what you were doing, which under time pressure loses to the known path.

Then the failed first attempt. A partner tries it once, in the spring, on a hard live task. It gives her something wrong or bland, she concludes it isn’t good enough for her work, and she is right on the day. What’s missing is the update path. Retesting costs what the first test cost, pays out uncertainly, and nothing will tell her the answer would be different now. Capability improves quietly and continuously. Beliefs update rarely and expensively.

Checking is the rest of it. On work that carries your name you can’t send what you haven’t read, so if reading it takes as long as writing it, the tool is a net loss and the person who quits is right. What survives is the narrow set of tasks where verification is nearly free: a document she was going to read anyway, a first draft she was going to rewrite regardless. Frequency decides the rest: five minutes saved every Tuesday sticks, three hours saved twice a year doesn’t. A tool gets used where verification is cheap relative to production, and where the moment to use it already exists in the day. Everything else has to be remembered and then checked.

About that dashboard. One major vendor defines an active user, in its own documentation, as somebody who used the product at least once in the previous 28 days. Once, in 28 days. If your adoption number came off a vendor dashboard, that’s the sentence underneath it, and bad evidence of time saved is its own subject. The much-quoted number for failed AI pilots rests on a few dozen interviews, so I skip that one too.

The part nobody says out loud

A partner with twenty years in the work has evaluation criteria calibrated on the old method, and against those criteria the new one genuinely looks bad. She reads a drafted clause and sees the five things wrong with it. A first-year reads it and sees a clause. Her judgment is correct, and it’s also the thing preventing her from adopting. In professional services the expertise is the product, so when she says the tool commoditizes the work she’s making a claim about the firm’s pricing. “It’s just a tool” answers a business question with a reassurance, and people notice.

The junior is running a different calculation and it isn’t shyness either. An associate who suspects that handing over the first draft will cost her the repetitions that build the judgment she’ll be assessed on in five years is describing a real risk. Her firm hasn’t decided how anyone learns this work once the first draft is free, and neither have I.

Then the arithmetic that never appears in an adoption survey. In a firm that bills time, a tool that removes hours removes revenue until the pricing changes, and the pricing hasn’t changed. An associate who writes the memo in two hours instead of six has a worse month, mechanically, and nobody has told her otherwise. She isn’t resisting anything. She’s reading her compensation plan correctly.

Which is why “our people are resistant to change” is a lazy diagnosis. It accounts for every possible observation, which means it accounts for none. Those same people took up electronic signatures and document comparison without a workstream, because the calculation obviously favored it and nobody called that change management.

What I do instead

Pick the task before the tool, and pick two at most. A good first task recurs weekly, is set off by something that already happens and isn’t under the person’s control, and is cheap enough to check that they can tell in a minute whether it’s right. Notice what that excludes: the highest-value task in the building is usually rare, high-stakes and expensive to verify. Start there and you get an excellent demonstration and no habit.

Train on live work. No sample data, no slides. The session doesn’t end until they’ve produced one output they actually use, sent or saved or pasted into the live document. Then write the trigger down before they leave, in their own words: when the closing checklist lands on Thursday, I put it through this before I read it. Thirty seconds, and it’s most of the difference between a habit and an intention.

Not the enthusiast, though. They’d adopt anyway, and everyone already discounts their opinion. Pick someone with standing in the work who is mildly skeptical, busy, and whose output other people see, because a senior person using it in the open lowers what using it costs everybody else.

Then the part almost everyone skips. Three weeks later, half an hour, on their real work again. Not a survey, not a dashboard. Four questions:

  1. When did you last use it? Show me.
  2. What did you do the last time you needed this and didn’t use it?
  3. What’s the annoying part?
  4. Has anyone asked you how you did it?

The first answers most of it before they speak. What turns up is small and boring. The output pastes with strange bullets, or it asked them to log in again and the password wasn’t to hand. Nobody files a ticket for that; they stop, and if you aren’t sitting beside them you never find out.

Telling a training problem from a correct answer

Start with whether anyone ever had a first successful use, on their own work, with someone beside them. If not, nothing has been tested; all you’ve learned is that busy professionals won’t teach themselves software mid-quarter.

If they cleared that bar and stopped anyway, ask what they do instead. The answer decides it. “I do it myself, the old way” points at verification cost or a missing trigger, and is often cheap to fix. “I use my own account on my phone” means the tool wasn’t rejected, your deployment was, usually because the thing you bought can’t see a single client document and the free one at least sees what they paste into it. That’s a permissions decision wearing a quality costume, and I’ve written about that. “I don’t do that task the way you assumed” means the task model was wrong, which belongs to whoever picked the task. Often that’s me.

The most common right answer is neither rollout nor cancellation. It’s three seats where you bought thirty. A tool can be exactly right for the two people who do that task all day and useless to everyone else. The firm-wide license exists because the vendor priced it that way, not because the work does.

Then ask it plainly: if we cancelled this tomorrow, who would notice inside a week? If nobody, cancel it. The craft detail is the date. Set the review before you buy: “eight weeks, and we turn it off if it hasn’t taken” is easy to execute, while the same call after a quarter of defending the spend in partner meetings is nearly impossible. By then it’s about the person who bought it, which is why he shouldn’t run the review. Firms keep paying for tools nobody opens because cancelling means somebody has to say out loud that the purchase was wrong. That’s a smaller admission than another year of license fees.

What working looks like

It looks disappointing from outside. Two tasks. One person. A recurring Thursday. Nothing to announce. The firms I’ve watched get something real out of the last two years mostly did one small thing and kept doing it. The ones that got nothing bought more things.

The part of this work that decides the outcome is showing up three weeks later and asking one person to open the tool and show me the last time they used it. Half an hour, and nothing anybody can put in a board pack. It’s also the only part I’ve ever seen work, and often enough it ends with me recommending they cancel the subscription and sending a shorter invoice than either of us expected.
