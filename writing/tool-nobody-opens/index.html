The tool nobody opens

When someone stops using a tool after training, look at the task they tried and what they do instead.

Open the tool’s history with someone who attended the training. If the last entry is the document they worked through in that session, the rollout has not yet established a habit. The account and the subscription may be fine. What you still need to understand is why the person returned to the old way of working.

A successful training exercise gives you a place to start that conversation. It does not tell you whether the tool is useful when the person is busy and working alone.

Why people stop

Sometimes the tool is a detour. The work lives in an inbox, document system, or spreadsheet, and using the assistant means leaving it. Under time pressure, the familiar path can be easier even when the tool would help with part of the task.

A failed first attempt can also last a long time in someone’s memory. A partner tries a difficult task, receives something wrong or bland, and decides the tool is not good enough for her work. Retesting requires another investment of time without a certain return. If the product improves, someone still needs a reason to revisit that decision.

Checking the output is part of the cost. If verifying and correcting a draft takes as long as writing it, there may be no saving. Tasks involving a document someone already knows can be easier to check. A recurring task also gives them more chances to learn when the tool is useful, though an occasional task can still justify keeping access.

Check what the dashboard counts as use. If one interaction in a 28-day window qualifies someone as active, attendance at a training session could look like adoption for weeks afterward. Neither that count nor the number of prompts establishes that a task got easier. Measuring the effect on work takes a separate comparison.

What adopting the tool asks of the person

A partner with twenty years of experience may see problems in a drafted clause that a first-year associate misses. Her concerns can be a useful assessment of the output. They also raise a training question: can the people using the tool recognize the errors she sees?

The junior has another reason to hesitate. Writing first drafts supplies repetitions that help build judgment. If the tool takes over that work, the firm needs another way for associates to practice. I do not have a settled answer for how much of that work can be delegated without losing something in training.

Then there is compensation. If an associate is assessed on billed hours, turning a six-hour memo into a two-hour memo can leave her worse off unless she has other billable work to fill the time or the assessment changes. Training her to use a tool does not resolve that incentive.

Calling all of this “resistance to change” gives you little to act on. Checking cost, opportunities to learn, and compensation each require a different response. Some of those responses belong to the partners running the firm rather than the person teaching the software.

Make the next use specific

Pick one or two tasks for the first training session. A weekly task with an obvious trigger gives someone a chance to repeat what they learned. Check that they can assess the output quickly enough to make using it worthwhile.

Work with material approved for the tool and an actual task the person needs to finish. Aim to leave the session with an output they have checked and can use. Then write down when they will try it again, in their own words: when the closing checklist lands on Thursday, I put it through this before I read it.

Include someone who is busy, knows the work, and has reservations about the tool. An enthusiast can help with training, but a skeptical colleague may identify obstacles the enthusiast is willing to work around. Their experience also gives other staff a more useful comparison.

Arrange a follow-up three weeks later, on their actual work. I use these questions:

  1. When did you last use it? Show me.
  2. What did you do the last time you needed this and didn’t use it?
  3. What’s the annoying part?
  4. Has anyone asked you how you did it?

The obstacles can be mundane. The output pastes with strange bullets. The tool asks for another login and the password is not to hand. Someone may return to the old method without filing a ticket. Watching the task gives you a chance to see what a survey misses.

Choose a response to what you find

If someone never completed a useful task, revisit the training and the task selection. If they completed one and then stopped, ask what they do instead.

“I do it myself” calls for a closer look at the cost of preparing inputs and checking outputs. “I use my own account on my phone” calls for a comparison of the approved setup with the one they chose. The approved tool may lack access to necessary documents; review the scope and permissions before assuming a difference in model quality.

“I don’t do that task the way you assumed” is a different problem. Whoever selected the task needs to revise the proposal. That can be me. Better training will not fix a workflow built around the wrong account of the job.

A smaller deployment may fit what you find. Two people who do a task all day can get value from a tool that the rest of the firm rarely needs. Review the seats against actual work rather than assuming everyone needs the same access.

For a tool intended for weekly work, ask who would notice if it disappeared next week. If nobody would, cancellation deserves consideration. For an occasional task, review the last relevant occasion instead; absence of weekly use is not enough to decide.

Set a review date before purchase and agree what would justify renewal. Where possible, include someone who did not choose the product. It is easier to discuss an unused subscription when the conversation is about the observed work and the agreed criteria.

After the follow-up, record the specific obstacle and the proposed response. If you change the setup or repeat the training, check the next time the task comes around. The useful outcome is knowing whether that change helped the person finish their work.
